## THE DUTCH SAFETY BOARD  
The Dutch Safety Board was set up to investigate and establish the actual or probable cause(s) of 
individual incidents or of categories of incidents in all sectors. The aim of such an investigation is 
solely to prevent such future accidents or incidents and to attach recommendations to them if the 
findings give cause to do so. The organisation consists of a Board with five permanent members 
and also has a number of standing committees. Special Guidance Committees are set up for 
specific investigations. The Safety Board is supported by a Bureau staffed by investigators, 
secretary reporters and a support staff.

## CONSIDERATIONS  
Three derailments 
On Monday 15 August 2005, the intercity train bound for Haarlem derailed in the western yard of 
Amsterdam Central station. No-one was injured but much property was damaged. This derailment 
was the third in the same yard within a short space of time, as on 6 June a loaded gravel-carrying 
train had already derailed, to be followed on 10 June by an empty coal train. The fact that three 
derailments had occurred within a short space of time in the same yard led the Dutch Safety Board 
to initiate an investigation. An important question here was the extent (if any) to which the yard 
itself caused, or was a contributing factor to, the three derailments. The investigation concluded 
that the local infrastructure did not have any shortcomings that may be deemed to have caused 
the three derailments. 
Complicated immediate cause for the third derailment  
In November of last year, the Dutch Safety Board published a report on the first two derailments. 
The immediate cause of the first derailment turned out to be a loose tyre; the second derailment 
proved to have been caused by errors in the repair work for the first derailment, resulting in a set 
of points failing to work properly.  
However, it took longer to complete the investigation into the derailment on 15 August 2005, as 
this derailment proved to be very complex. There were a host of factors that played a role in this 
incident, without a single one of these factors being decisive in causing it. 
The rearmost bogie of the carriage in front of the pushing locomotive was the first to derail, doing 
so towards the inner side of the bend. However, it was no longer possible to say exactly why the 
carriage derailed there and in that way. After extensive investigation, the Dutch Safety Board has 
established that there was no single cause that can explain the derailment on its own. Instead, it is 
a combination of the following factors:  
 
layout of the infrastructure (tight bend, short straight section, tight reserve curve)  
 
significant pushing force  
 
length of the train, and 
 
its speed.

The question in this context is the extent to which the above factors explain the cause of the 
derailment. The answer is they do and they don’t. They do explain it, because the simulations 
performed show that together, the above factors create a peak in the forces that lie close to the 
derailment thresholds at the exact place where the train actually derailed. This means that the 
derailment can be very largely explained by a combination of the above factors. And they don’t 
explain it, because it proved impossible to determine the precise influence exerted by each 
individual factor. The possible existence of another factor over and above the factors mentioned 
above cannot be discounted either. After the event, the Dutch Safety Board weighed up all the 
options for other factors as well as it could. After some investigation, we directed our attention to 
the buffers. However, the Board was unable to demonstrate a direct link between the faults found 
and the cause of the derailment. 
In the simulations performed, the train nearly derails, but not quite. There are two possible 
explanations for this, namely that either the simulation’s limitations mean that when it uses the 
above factors the train just avoids derailment although it does derail in practice, or alternatively 
that there were factors that have remained undiscovered and thus have not been incorporated into 
the simulation but that actually did affect events in practice. However, the Dutch Safety Board feels 
that the latter possibility is not very likely. It proved possible to exclude the most likely factors. 
The only possibility that could not be excluded was that of a failure of the pushing and traction 
gear (incl. the buffers) between carriages. However, if the failure of buffers is incorporated into the 
simulations, this does not significantly affect the likelihood of derailment. The Dutch Safety Board 
believes that the first explanation is the more likely. Simulations are by definition a simplification of 
reality. Note too that it is not exactly clear what the limits of applicability of the models used in 
railway simulations are and hence how reliable the results are.

Based on the established facts and the simulations based on them, the Dutch Safety Board has 
come to the conclusion that the combination of the above factors can explain the derailment. 
However, the Board was unable to determine whether all aspects of all relevant factors had been 
fully taken into account, although it was able to determine that the combination of factors - namely 
the infrastructure (incl. the tight bends), the (long, pushed) train, and the pushing force and speed 
of the train - created a risky situation. With some exaggeration, it may be said that the most 
dangerous type of train rode over the most dangerous part of the track. Although it was a fairly 
unique combination of factors, it could occur again. This is why the Dutch Safety Board, writing in 
its interim report (November 2006), advised NSR (NS Reizigers) and ProRail to take measures to 
prevent this type of train from riding over a similar piece of track again. In October 2007, NSR and 
ProRail informed us that they had taken measures to prevent repetition of such an occurrence.  
Added value of report 
The Dutch Safety Board wonders how well the most important lesson of the derailment has been 
learned. The Board believes that the investigation still provides enough new and important 
information to justify the publication of a report, even more than two years after the event. This 
important information mainly relates to the admission procedure for the pushed train. The 
simulation results that indicated the train was close to the derailment thresholds had led NS 
Reizigers and the other stakeholders to conclude that it was safe to deploy the train. The 
derailment has shown that this was not the case. The Dutch Safety Board has concluded that 
simulation models that attempt to evaluate derailment safety are still too limited to be fully 
trustworthy in those sorts of conditions.  
In addition to conclusions that relate more to technical issues, the Dutch Safety Board, with one 
eye on the derailment and the investigation, needs to go into the responsibilities of the 
stakeholders. The Board has established that the stakeholders did not fulfil their own responsibility 
for safety in the correct way and that the relationship between these different responsibilities was 
not always clear. Partly due to its experiences from other investigations, the Dutch Safety Board is 
of the opinion that it is essential to properly coordinate and implement safety-related 
responsibilities. The Board wishes to make the following comments on the responsibilities of the 
stakeholders. 
NS Reizigers  
NS Reizigers is responsible for the safety of passengers and thus for the investigation into the 
safety of new or modified trains. NSR has taken on its responsibility by commissioning an 
investigation into the safe deployability of this train, and asked for recommendations from the 
infrastructure administrator (ProRail). This approach is in line with the Dutch Safety Board’s own 
safety policy. 
NSR concluded from the results of the simulations performed that the train could be safely 
deployed. The simulations were not based on the actual state of the track. Even so, even with a 
better track design the simulation results come out close to the set limits. The Dutch Safety Board 
regrets that this did not spur NSR to carry out more extensive practical tests to find out whether a 
less favourable set of circumstances could cause the train to derail in practice. The need for such 
tests was all the more apparent given the warnings of the risks involved that have been received 
from Switzerland. The Swiss recommended that sufficient practical tests be carried out before the 
long pushed trains were introduced into the Netherlands. The Dutch Safety Board believes that in 
future NSR must take a more critical look at simulation results, because it bears the ultimate 
responsibility for passenger safety. 
In the Dutch Safety Board’s opinion, NS Reizigers’ responsibility also means that it (NSR) must 
tackle ProRail about the data that the infrastructure administrator provides for the admission 
testing. In this case, NSR accepted fairly unquestioningly that ProRail provided more favourable 
information on the track than was justified by the track’s actual state at that time. It is certainly 
true in respect of the sets of points that NSR knew that in reality the quality of the points on the 
tracks was poorer than indicated by ProRail. In a certain sense, NSR is entitled to trust ProRail to 
provide accurate information. However, if NSR, using its own expertise and experience - including 
that of its engine drivers - knows or suspects that in practice the situation is worse than the 
situation depicted by ProRail, then it must tackle ProRail about this. 
ProRail 
ProRail is responsible for the safety of the infrastructure. In the Dutch Safety Board’s opinion, this 
certainly includes providing accurate reports on the actual state of the infrastructure. However,

ProRail failed to do this. The Dutch Safety Board has had to find out for itself that ProRail does not 
have enough information about the actual state of the rails. ProRail does not have precise 
information on the exact dimensions of the radius of the reserve curve or the length of the straight 
section, nor could it provide information in time on any other sites of tight bends and (too) short 
straight sections. Given the importance of accurate information on the infrastructure, this is an 
alarming situation. The Dutch Safety Board had already reported in a previous report (Ontsporing 
goederentrein bij Apeldoorn op 30 april 2003/Derailment of a goods train near Apeldoorn on 30 
April 2003), that there was a lack of information on the state of the infrastructure. In 2006, the 
TWMI (Transport and Water Management Inspectorate) came to the same conclusion after 
reviewing the safety management system used by ProRail, and instructed ProRail to rectify any 
shortcomings in this field by 1 January 2007. The Dutch Safety Board believes it is the 
responsibility of the TWMI to ensure that ProRail has actually done this in practice.  
TWMI  
Finally, the Dutch Safety Board would like to go into the role played by the TWMI (Transport and 
Water Management Inspectorate). The Inspectorate has issued a statement about the admission of 
the train as a whole. However, the only statutory requirement related to the admission of the 
steering carriage1, as the locomotive and the carriages had already been approved. The fact that in 
this case the Inspectorate acted pro-actively could be viewed positively. However, the Dutch Safety 
Board sees things differently. The fact that at the end of the process the Inspectorate publishes its 
own opinion carries with it the risk that consciously or unconsciously the responsible parties 
(primary responsibility: NS Reizigers; secondary responsibility: ProRail) will try to shirk some of 
their own responsibilities, passing the buck to the Inspectorate instead. And this is in fact what 
happened in practice. In its correspondence with the Dutch Safety Board, one of the arguments 
NSR used in its defence was that the Inspectorate had given its approval and that as a result NSR 
was entitled to assume that it was actually safe to deploy the train. The Dutch Safety Board is of 
the opinion that a transport provider is itself responsible for determining whether it is safe to 
deploy a particular train and that it should not hide behind the Inspectorate’s opinion if something 
happens. This is similar to the situation in the construction sector, where the Dutch Safety Board 
has found that in some cases clients and supervisors do not guarantee the safety of the 
construction themselves but instead rely on the fact that a building permit has been issued. A 
building permit is not intended to be a guarantee of safety; moreover, it never absolves clients and 
supervisors from their own responsibility for safety. This is why the Dutch Safety Board is of the 
opinion that an Inspectorate must be very cautious about making official statements that are not 
required by law.  
Concluding remarks  
Taking everything into consideration, the Dutch Safety Board is of the opinion that – quite apart 
from the technical lessons already referred to – in future all the stakeholders must be more critical 
when implementing their own responsibilities for safety in practice. The Dutch Safety Board 
reached a similar conclusion in the investigation into the folding of the ship ‘No Limit’ in the 
IJmuiden locks. That case related to a vessel design that had been modified so much from previous 
designs used for similar vessels that in practice it was a new design. The testing techniques used 
were geared towards traditionally designed ships but proved unusable for this type of ship. If the 
stakeholders had fulfilled their own responsibilities better in practice, this problem would have been 
noticed and the incident might have been prevented.  
All transport sectors have seen developments such as new vehicle designs - in the case of the 
railway sector, think of the so-called ‘light-rail’ concept -, new draft regulations that only apply to 
new circumstances, and so on. Here, transport providers bear the primary responsibility for 
ensuring that passengers in particular can be transported safely. Infrastructure administrators bear 
their own responsibility for publishing clear criteria that the infrastructure imposes on the vehicles 
to be used within it. This makes it essential to have detailed knowledge of the infrastructure. 
Additionally, in the Dutch Safety Board’s opinion, when supervising safety the TWMI should refrain 
from taking over responsibilities from transport providers and other parties. However, in a general 
sense the Inspectorate must form an opinion about the way in which transport providers and 
notified bodies are fulfilling their own responsibilities for admission in practice.

It is true for all parties that after an accident each must singly and collectively take on board the 
relevant lessons from the accident. In this case it was NS Reizigers and ProRail in particular who 
blamed each other, without it being clear exactly what had happened. This is why there was an 
initial failure to take on board all the lessons to be learned from the derailment. In the meantime, 
the stakeholders have shown that they have learned more than was initially the case. The Dutch 
Safety Board hopes and expects that this report will help to further improve the way in which the 
parties fulfil their responsibility for the safety of the Dutch railways. 
## RECOMMENDATIONS 
1.  We recommend that NS Reizigers ensure that when a new or modified train is introduced, the 
evaluation of the risks is based on the actual state of the track and on scientifically reliable 
practical tests/measurements (and not just on unsubstantiated or insufficiently substantiated 
simulation results). 
2.  We recommend that ProRail draw up a national list of all the places in the railway network that 
do not comply with current design requirements and make transparent decisions about the way 
in which these shortcomings are to be dealt with. ProRail must also ensure that shortcomings 
such as the actual state of the track are incorporated into admission tests. Especially important 
in this respect is data on the bend radii and on the straight sections between bends. 
3. We recommend that the Transport and Water Management Inspectorate refrain from making 
official statements about specific admissions if a statement is not required by law. However, we 
do recommend that the Inspectorate come to a general opinion about the equipment admission 
procedures used by transport providers and notified bodies, and in particular about the 
soundness of the basic principles and investigation methods used.

## INTRODUCTION 
## THE INCIDENT 
1.1 
On Monday 15 August 2005, an Intercity train bound for Haarlem derailed in the western yard of 
Amsterdam Central station. The train was being pushed. The locomotive, which was pushing twelve 
carriages, was being operated from the front carriage2. As the result of a derailment, the pushing 
locomotive went on to a different track than the one that the preceding carriages were taking. As a 
result, the rearmost carriage ended up standing crossways between the locomotive and the 
preceding carriage (see illustration 1). None of the 200 passengers on board at the time - 30 of 
whom were in the severely damaged carriage in front of the locomotive - were injured, although 
there was much damage to property. The aerial contact line collapsed along the entire length of the 
western yard of Amsterdam Central station.

1.2 
This was the third derailment in a short space of time in the same yard, as a gravel-carrying train 
had already derailed there on 6 June 2005, followed a few days later on 10 June by an empty 
goods train.  
The fact that three derailments occurred shortly after one another in this yard raised the issue of 
the extent (if any) to which the yard itself was the sole or joint reason for the three derailments. 
This was why the Dutch Safety Board decided to launch an investigation. 
No common cause  
Each derailment was investigated to see (amongst other things) the extent (if any) to which 
shortcomings in the infrastructure of the yard located on the western side of Amsterdam Central 
station had contributed to the derailments. Neither the investigation into the first two derailments

Investigation into the third derailment 
The investigation into the derailment on 15 August 2005 took longer, mainly because it was a 
difficult and very complicated matter to determine the immediate cause of the derailment. There 
turned out to be a number of factors that had played a role, with it proving impossible to point the

However, by November 2006 the investigation into the third derailment was so far advanced that it

a long pushed train,  
the delivery of a maximum pushing force by the locomotive in a points line4, and

Each of these factors on its own generates great forces (namely lateral forces, forces between 
buffers and/or horizontal forces) which, when aggregated, are such that there is a risk of 
derailment. This combination of factors can still occur, which is why the Dutch Safety Board 
decided to send an interim report to NS Reizigers and ProRail in order to warn them about this 
combination of factors and to advise them to take suitable measures. The letter accompanying the 
report was sent on 30 November 2006 (see the letter in Appendix 2). See Section 5.5 for a 
description of the measures that the stakeholders have taken. 
Focus of this report 
The Dutch Safety Board is of the opinion that - in addition to the points set out in the interim 
report - there are important lessons to be learned from the investigation into the derailment on 15 
August 2005, these lessons primarily relating to the admission of the train in 2003 and to the 
infrastructure design. Our findings were reason enough to publish this report and to make 
recommendations.

## FACTUAL INFORMATION ABOUT THE INCIDENT  
On 15 August 2005 just after 9.00 a.m., a passenger train derailed in the western yard of 
Amsterdam Central station. This was the Intercity train IC-822 that had left Maastricht at about 
6.29 a.m. and was en route for Haarlem via Eindhoven, Den Bosch, Utrecht and Amsterdam. 
The circumstances of this accident were investigated by the Transport and Water Management 
Inspectorate, rail supervision unit (‘Inspectie Verkeer en Waterstaat, Toezichteenheid Rail’), by 
ProRail and by the transport company involved (NS Reizigers)6. Following the Dutch Safety Board’s 
own investigation and the above-mentioned reports, the Board believes that the most important 
facts surrounding this accident are as follows: 
Shortly before its derailment, the train had been positioned alongside platform 5, in line with the 
timetable. After the usual departure procedure, the train departed at about 9.07 a.m. after having 
been given the signal position ‘green’. At that time, about 200 people were on board. The ARI 
(automatic route-setting system) was used to set a route from track 5A via track 31 to track SB 
(see illustration 2), this route requiring eleven sets of points to be passed.

## DUTCH LEGISLATION  
3.1 
The main piece of Dutch legislation relating to railway transportation is the 2003 Railways Act, 
which came into force on 1 January 2005. This Act lays down the main responsibilities of the 
various parties within the railway transportation sector. Features:  
 
The infrastructure must comply with further requirements to be laid down by the Minister 
 
The minister will award one or more concessions for the administration of the main railway 
infrastructure. 
 
A railway company will be entitled to access the main railways if it has been awarded an 
operating permit and safety certificate, both of which are awarded by the minister. 
 
Railway equipment must comply with further requirements to be set.  
 
The minister will appoint one or more supervisory authorities by decree.  
Articles 36 to 48 of the Railways Act relate to the admission of railway vehicles to the main railway 
network (i.e. to their approval). The specific admission requirements are laid down in the 
legislation known as the Besluit keuring spoorvoertuigen (Decree on the certification of railway 
vehicles) and the Regeling keuring spoorvoertuigen (Regulation on the certification of railway 
vehicles). The owner of a railway vehicle is responsible for obtaining an admission certificate and a 
deployment certificate. The admission certificate relates to the technical requirements for the 
equipment. The owner must bring in an agency (a ‘notified body’) recognised by the minister to 
test the equipment to be admitted, in order to ensure that it complies with the statutory 
requirements. If the equipment does comply then the notified body will issue an admission 
certificate. The Transport and Water Management Inspectorate supervises the notified bodies. A 
deployment certificate relates to a test of whether the equipment is also suitable for use on the 
Dutch railways. The Transport and Water Management Inspectorate issues this deployment 
certificate. To obtain it, the owner must himself commission testing of the equipment and obtain 
advice from the infrastructure administrator. To obtain a deployment certificate, you need an 
admission certificate and proof of compliance with requirements relating to the compatibility of the 
vehicle with the Dutch railway infrastructure. 
The new Railways Act had not yet come into force at the time the derailed train was admitted. The 
standards that applied at that time are set out in the following subsection. Basically, they are in 
line with the standards that have now been included in the Railways Act. However, one major 
difference related to the lack of notified bodies. At the time the train in question was admitted, the 
admissions testing was performed by external agencies commissioned by the equipment owner. 
The Transport and Water Management Inspectorate submitted an independent overall assessment 
of the testing - if its assessment was favourable, it would award the admission certificate. Officially, 
this admission certificate is only required for new or modified equipment, which in this case only 
related to the steering carriage.  
In the event of modifications to existing equipment, the Transport and Water Management 
Inspectorate was authorised to perform an abbreviated admission procedure that specifically 
looked at the modifications made. If an abbreviated or provisional admission procedure was 
performed, the TWMI would issue a VGB (certificate of no objection). 
The Regeling infrastructuur (Regulation on the infrastructure) stipulates that the infrastructure 
administrator is responsible for ensuring that the ratio between the horizontal and vertical forces 
acting on the railway rails must be lower than 0.8. This value is defined in an international 
standard (UIC 518) as a threshold value for susceptibility to derailment.

## STANDARDS AND GUIDELINES 
3.2 
A feature of the railway sector is that a large number of specific guidelines have been drawn up by 
the industry itself - prior to 1995 by Nederlandse Spoorwegen (the railway transport provider) but 
since then by various parties.  
Prior to the coming into force of the 2003 Railways Act, ProRail’s ontwerpvoorschrift wissels en 
kruisingen (draft regulation on sets of points and line crossings) (OVS00056-6.1) applied. 
However, the draft regulations only apply to new infrastructure. The draft regulations may only be 
deviated from with the permission of Pro-Rail’s Manager Technisch-Economisch-Beheer. The draft 
regulations include requirements relating to minimum radii and to minimum straight sections in S-
bends.  
Two standards apply to the admission of trains to the Dutch railway network: 
 
M-001: admission requirements relating to railway rolling stock safety; 
 
M-005: procedure for the admission of equipment to the national railway network. 
These two standards do not contain any specific rules for using pushed trains, although there is a 
reference to international standards that contain general rules that all trains must comply with. 
These rules include threshold values for the forces between wheel and rail (UIC 518). A distinction 
is drawn here between threshold values for susceptibility to derailment and threshold values for 
trackload. The most important threshold value for susceptibility to derailment is the ratio between 
horizontal and vertical forces between wheel and rail (the so-called ‘Y/Q value’). The threshold 
value for this is 0.8. The threshold value for trackload relates to the maximum lateral force that a 
wheel may apply to the rail, and depends on the actual conditions. Note that if this value is 
exceeded, this does not mean that there is an immediate risk of derailment, although there is an 
indirect relationship with safety levels, as if this value is exceeded there will more likely be wear 
and tear to the track.  
Each transport provider must obtain a safety certificate, which is awarded by the Transport and 
Water Management Inspectorate on the Minister’s behalf. The most important requirement for 
obtaining a safety certificate is to have a properly working safety assurance system. At the time of 
admission, the requirements that a safety assurance system had to comply with were laid down in 
TWMI standard V-001. This standard lays down requirements for safety policy, risk inventories and 
evaluation, for their implementation and for monitoring, for safety tests and for the corrective 
action to be taken. At that time, the procedure for obtaining a safety certificate was laid down in 
standard V-002. A certificate is issued if a document analysis, a management presentation and a 
practical assessment are deemed to be satisfactory. As stated, this admission procedure is now 
regulated by the new Railways Act, which amongst other things refers to European standards 
including Safety Decree 04/49/EC.  
## ASSESSMENT FRAMEWORK FOR SAFETY MANAGEMENT 
3.3 
In principle, there are several possible ways of testing and assessing the way in which an 
organisation fulfils its own responsibility for safety in practice. This also means that there is no 
universal guide that can be used in all circumstances. This is despite the fact that since the 1990s 
there has been an increasing focus on own responsibility for safety. Accordingly, the Dutch Safety 
Board has itself selected five safety issues that give some idea of the factors that could be 
important and their relative importance. The Dutch Safety Board believes that this selection is 
justified, because these safety-related action items have been incorporated into numerous pieces

accepted and broadly implemented standards. In fact, a safety management system that 
specifically relates to the railway sector has been incorporated into European Directive 04/49/EC

correspond to the action items formulated by the Dutch Safety Board.  
The Dutch Safety Board has selected the following five action items (NB. For further details, see 
Section 3):

## ANALYSIS 
## GENERAL POINTS 
5.1 
As already stated, after the derailment a full investigation was carried out by the Dutch Safety 
Board, TWMI, ProRail and NS Reizigers. The Dutch Safety Board concludes from these  
investigations that the main cause of the derailment was not a technical fault in the train’s 
equipment (such as a defective wheel or jammed axle11) or a technical fault in the infrastructure 
equipment (such as a broken or severely sagging rail). 
For this reason, it is the Dutch Safety Board’s opinion that we must proceed on the basis that the 
immediate cause was a combination of factors. And, as already stated in the previous section, we 
have found indications for this, namely that the train was travelling at a higher speed than 
permitted at that spot, that it was a pushed train of unusually long length12, that said train was 
being driven through a points line with a relatively high number of tight sets of points, and that the 
train was accelerating even though most of it was still travelling through the points line. 
An investigation was carried out into the layout of the relevant section of the points line. Section 
5.2 looks at this investigation, with more details being included in Appendix 4. The subsequent 
investigation by the Dutch Safety Board focused on uncovering the combination of factors that had 
caused this derailment. Simulations are the only way to obtain a better understanding of the 
degree to which the factors individually and collectively contributed to the derailment. The 
simulation took into account both train-related factors (speed, pushing force, buffer failure etc.) 
and infrastructural factors (incl. track layout). The results of the simulation are summarised in 
Section 5.3, with Appendix 5 providing a more detailed account. The unusual configuration of the 
train and the fact that this configuration had only recently been permitted led us to carry out 
further investigation into the admission process (see Section 5.4 and Appendix 6). Section 5.5 
looks at the measures taken after the derailment.

## ANALYSIS OF THE IMMEDIATE CAUSE: SIMULATIONS 
5.3 
5.3.1 
General 
The Dutch Safety Board used a simulation model in its investigations and analysis of the immediate 
cause of the derailment - for more information, see Appendix 5. A simulation model uses 
mathematical models to drive a computer-generated train along a computer-generated section of 
track. Data on the characteristic features of the train (including the springs in the sets of wheels, 
the buffers etc.) and the characteristic features of the track (radii, banking15 etc.) that is as 
accurate as possible is entered in the model. The results of a simulation are the data on the various 
dynamic forces (vertical forces between wheel and rail, horizontal forces etc.) that occurred in the 
relevant simulation, as well as data on the movements of the train as a whole and of the individual 
sections in relation to one another. What can then be done is to alter one set of parameters in a 
subsequent simulation whilst keeping the other parameters unchanged, so that the effect of the 
changed parameter on the dynamic forces can be investigated. Simulation results provide useful 
information on the contribution made by individual factors to the derailment. However, it must be 
remembered that simulations simplify reality, which means that although the results may give a 
good indication of the forces occurring, they are not proof of the precise values that occurred in 
reality.  
The reliability of the results can be improved by validating the model using measurements from 
practical tests. The simulation model used has been validated using measurements from practical 
tests that were carried out whilst the train was still at an experimental stage. However, only limited 
measurements were performed in respect of the derailment criteria in particular, which means that 
the validation tests too could only be performed to a limited extent. The results of the validation 
process indicate that in essence the model used gives similar results to the measurements obtained 
in practice. The validation is too limited to quantify the reliability and accuracy of the simulation 
model. 
Findings16 
5.3.2 
The simulations indicate that the pushed driving of this train along this points line generated great 
lateral forces and high wheel load throw-off. The high wheel load throw-off occurs on the left-hand 
side of the spot where the train actually derailed on the left-hand side. Taken together, these 
factors create a critical situation, with the forces coming close to exceeding the internationally 
agreed threshold values for railway safety.  
There are two factors that, in combination, created a critical situation:  
1. pushing a long train with (almost) maximum force, this generating high longitudinal forces; 
2. driving through a sharp bend that was followed almost immediately by a sharp reserve curve; 
when combined with the first factor this creates extra large transverse forces, high wheel load 
throw-off and extra large buffer forces. 
There are also a number of additional factors that can make the situation even more critical: 
the train speed17

 
a greater friction coefficient between buffers as the result of damage or poor lubrication; 
metals limiting movement between carriage body and bogie18 (leading to the bogie and the 
 
carriage body ‘banging’ against each other in tight bends); 


close to derailment thresholds, this does not mean by itself that we can say that there is an unsafe 
situation. However, if simulation calculations generate values that lie very close to the derailment 
thresholds, in the Dutch Safety Board’s opinion we must call this a critical situation. In practice, the 
values could actually lie at or exceed the derailment thresholds. In addition, the accuracy of the 
data on the state of the infrastructure entered in the model will affect the simulation results. In this 
case, some of the infrastructure parameters were not precisely known. The data on the railway 
gauge and the banking was known, but no data on the precise length of the straight section and 
the radii (for instance) was available. 
Failure of the buffer 
After the derailment, it was found that the left buffer of the carriage in front of the first derailed 
carriage was partially broken off (see illustration 8). Part of the damage noted to the buffers 
between the two carriages in front of the locomotive can be explained as consequential damage. 
One of the bolts used to attach the buffer plate to the carriage was found in two pieces. The marks 
on these pieces indicate a fracture as the result of overloading, which is further evidence for the 
theory that the buffer probably broke off as the result of the derailment. However, (rust) marks 
were found too, indicating a longer-term fault. One of the bolts in the link between the buffer and 
the carriage was either missing altogether or else was very loose. It is not entirely impossible that 
the buffer plate (i.e. the link between the buffer and the carriage) may have partially broken off at 
or just before the moment of derailment. This is because at that moment there is a high eccentric 
load20 on the buffer that has not been optimally secured. At that moment, both the relative buffer 
displacement (approx. 50 cm.) and the force between the buffers (200 to 300 kN) are almost at a 
maximum. The buffers in the other buffer pair are not touching each other at that moment, so that 
the entire force is transmitted through the first buffer pair. It may be that at that moment the 
relevant buffer partially detached itself, which may have created a dynamic effect that contributed 
to the derailment. However, the most logical explanation is that the buffer broke off as the result of 
the derailment.  
Quite apart from the breaking off of a buffer, the high eccentric load can also lock a buffer in the 
sense that it can only be pushed in a little way. Such a locking can in turn mean that other, 
potentially greater forces are exchanged via the pull/push mechanism between the carriages.  
Both the locking and the breaking off of a buffer were incorporated into the simulation. This 
produced data indicating that the breaking off or locking creates somewhat greater fluctuations in 
the forces between wheel and rail21. In a risky situation this will increase the risk of derailment, but 
there is no decisive effect. It should be noted here too that when simulating the breaking off of a 
buffer the limits of the simulation model are being reached, which means that these results must 
be treated with caution.

values (this being a measure of the risk of derailment) and high wheel load throw-off.  
Simulations are a simplified representation of reality. The exact location of all items in the 
infrastructure was not known, which reduces the reliability of the results. Note too that the model 
used has not been validated so much that we can state how accurately the results correspond to 
the forces occurring in reality. What we do know from the limited validation that we have is that 
the levels of forces (and in particular the lateral forces) basically correspond. The values found in 
the simulation for the risk of derailment lie close to the maximum permitted values. Given the 
uncertainty outlined above regarding the simulation results, it is realistic to assume that in practice 
the maximum values could well be exceeded and/or that little would need to occur for the train to 
derail. This is why the Dutch Safety Board refers to a ‘risky situation’.  
This risk should not be ascribed solely to the train or to the infrastructure but instead to a 
combination of these two factors. With a probability bordering on certainty, it may be argued that 
the train would not have derailed if:  
 
it had been pulled instead of pushed;   
 
or had been pushed through a points line with broader curves and longer straight sections;  
 
or had not been pushed with (almost) maximum pushing force but had been rolling freely 
through the points line.

## ADMISSIONS 
5.4 
Admission procedure22 
5.4.1 
In 1999, NS Reizigers launched a project to introduce pull-push23 Intercity trains (i.e. ones that can 
be both pulled and pushed) onto the Maastricht-Haarlem line. The most important reason for this 
was the efficiency gains compared to the use of trains that can only be pulled. This was because 
the latter require the locomotive to be swapped at each start station and end station, which 
shunting requires time, additional personnel and additional locomotives at the starting point and 
final destination, none of which additional resources are required for a pull-push train.  
Between 1999 and 2003, NS Reizigers mandated Nedtrain Consulting24 (NTC) to carry out research 
into the possible deployment of a pull-push train. The train consists of a locomotive (type 1700) at 
one end of the train, a steering carriage at the other end and eleven carriages in between. The 
locomotive and the carriages were already being used and thus had already been admitted for use 
on the railway. However, an admission certificate did still have to be obtained from the TWMI for 
the steering carriage. NSR also asked NTC to research the safety issues relating to the pushing of a 
long train.  
NSR has experience with pushed trains. For example, the Benelux train had already been deployed 
as a pull-push train since the 1970s with the same Intercity carriages, although note that the 
Benelux train is much shorter (205 metres compared to 340 metres), lighter (370 tonnes 
compared to 570 tonnes) and uses a less powerful locomotive.  
In 1999, NTC visited Swiss Rail to obtain more information on the long pushed trains that they 
were using. These discussions revealed that using long pushed trains is not without risk and that 
such trains should not be introduced until extensive practical testing had been carried out. In 
Switzerland, many modifications had been made to the long trains to permit safe pushing, such as 
reinforcing the buffers, having the buffers attached more solidly to the vehicle housing, and 
restricting the locomotive’s pushing power. 
Although NTC carried out practical tests on a pushed train with twelve carriages that was being 
driven over two 1:8 sets of points, these tests mainly looked at levels of comfort and wear and tear 
and hardly considered the risk of derailment at all. NTC then carried out simulation tests. The 
simulation model was validated using the results of the practical tests. The simulation model was 
based on two 1:9 sets of points. As the result of its simulation tests, in March 2002 NTC came to 
the conclusion that the introduction of a pushed train with 12 carriages would not be safe unless 
additional measures were implemented. A number of safety norms were exceeded, both in the 
practical tests and in the simulations. NTC recommended that either the number of carriages be 
limited to 8 or that the pushing force of the locomotive when driving through points lines be limited 
to 60%. As an alternative, NTC also mentioned the possibility of making structural modifications to 
the wheel sets and of drawing up standards for the quality of the infrastructure that the trains 
used.  
Following on from these initial results, NTC, after consulting with NSR and ProRail, carried out new 
simulations, using a longer interim straight section between the sets of points. According to 
ProRail, the straight section length first used by NTC (2.3 metres) was not realistic, so a length of 
12 metres - the OVS standard straight section length - was used instead. Simulations that used 
this longer straight section yielded significantly more favourable results. Based on these new 
simulation results, NTC concluded that Intercity equipment with a train length of up to 12 carriages 
could safely be deployed in pull/push mode. The standards for the risk of derailment were not 
exceeded. NTC deemed the exceeding of the maximum permitted lateral rail forces25 by 2.5% to be 
marginal and acceptable, as it was based on the least favourable scenario.

Evaluation 
Admission process 
The legislation does not lay down an official admission procedure for the modified deployment of a 
previously admitted train. Officially, admission was only required for the steering carriage. All the 
same, NSR decided to carry out an investigation into the effects of pushing a train with 12 
carriages and to submit the results to ProRail and the TWMI. Apart from this, the admission 
process proceeded in line with the applicable rules (see Normblad (standard sheet) M-005 by the 
TWMI). However, the Dutch Safety Board is of the opinion that the Inspectorate would have done 
better to restrict itself to the admission of the steering carriage and that the Inspectorate should 
not have issued a statement about the train as a whole, as this can create the impression of joint 
responsibility, whereas it is in fact NS Reizigers that is primarily responsible for the safe 
deployment of the equipment. 
Admission testing too limited in scope 
The Dutch Safety Board primarily objects to certain aspects of the admission process. In the 
Board’s opinion, the admission test did not take into account the warnings already received about 
the risks of using a long pushed train. Note the following in this respect:

A) In the initial phase of the admission testing process, it became clear that the pushed driving of 
a long train is not without risk. This was established by NSR after a fact-finding mission to 
Switzerland. In addition, the initial simulation results indicated there was an unacceptable risk 
of derailment. While it’s true that these initial calculations were based on an unrealistic 
assumption of the length of the straight section between two sets of points28, they were also an 
indication that caution was required.  
B) The simulations used in the admission process are based on assumptions that are more 
favourable than the actual state of the track. First of all, this is true of the straight section 
between two opposing bends (in this case: sets of points). After observing that the initial 
assumption of the length of the straight section between two sets of points (2.3 metres) was 
unrealistic, a simulation was performed using a straight section length of 12 metres, this being 
the standard section length taken from the OVS draft regulations. However, the draft 
regulations only apply to new infrastructure. This means that older infrastructure containing 
shorter straight sections may (and does) continue to be used, as can be seen from the 
situation in Amsterdam and elsewhere, where the straight section between the bend and the 
reserve curve was less than 6 metres. When simulating the worst case scenario, the shortest 
straight section occurring in practice should have been used. The same reasoning applies to the 
sets of points. Simulations were performed using the 1:9 sets of points (the ‘tightest’ set of 
points cited in the draft regulations) but there are several 1:8 sets of points in use, especially 
in Amsterdam. These are ‘tighter’ (i.e. smaller radius and shorter straight section) and 
accordingly generate greater forces than do 1:9 sets of points. In other words, the simulations 
should have been based on these 1:8 sets of points. 
C)  The most important objection that the Dutch Safety Board has relates to the high esteem the 
simulation results were held in. The simulation results gave high y/q values (this being a 
measure of the risk of derailment). The maximum y/q value in the initial simulations is 0.77 
and in the second (which used the longer straight section) 0.71, whereas the standard has 
been set at 0.8. If the resulting values had been based on scientifically sound practical tests 
then it would have been established that the standard was not going to be exceeded and 
accordingly that the train could safely be deployed. However, simulation results are not the 
same as practical tests. Although the simulation model was indeed validated by a number of 
practical measurements, these measurements actually looked at standards of comfort and 
wear and tear, instead of explicitly at these derailment criteria (this being y/q, the ratio of 
vertical and horizontal forces between wheel and rail). In the Dutch Safety Board’s opinion, of 
all values it is the high y/q values that should have made the stakeholders cautious and should 
not have been used as proof of the safe nature of the train configuration to be deployed. In the 
Dutch Safety Board’s opinion, additional practical tests should have been performed, in order to 
obtain better information on the precise y/q values. 
To sum up, the Dutch Safety Board is of the opinion that the risks uncovered by the admission 
process were not investigated or weighed up properly. There are a number of underlying reasons 
for this: 
Safety management at NS Reizigers  
An underlying reason for the failure to properly investigate and assess risks is the limited focus of 
NS Reizigers’ safety management system (SMS). The procedures in the SMS are limited to the 
obtaining of an admission certificate and to complying with the applicable rules. The SMS does not 
prescribe a thorough risk inventarisation process for the deployment of new equipment or for the 
modified deployment of existing equipment, even though thorough risk inventarisation is essential 
in order to determine the consequences of the new or modified deployment on engine driver 
training, on equipment maintenance, and so on. A thorough risk inventarisation would have 
mapped out all risks and would have investigated and evaluated each of them individually. What 
has happened now is that unforeseen risks have arisen, such as potential problems with buffers, 
derailment risk caused by the locomotive’s pushing force and by tight bends. However, these risks 
were not systematically investigated and weighed up. The Dutch Safety Board has found no

issues were indeed discussed and included in the considerations and simply not put down on paper. 
Given the established exceeding of the standards, the distribution of the test results and the train’s 
unique configuration, the Dutch Safety Board believes that there should have been a detailed,

Conclusion 
None of the stakeholders assessed the safety issues relating to this train on the basis of a thorough 
risk analysis. The parties primarily based their assessment on simulation results and on limited 
practical tests. However valuable these may be, when a new type of train is being introduced – 
which in addition was a pushed train of an unprecedented length – in the Dutch Safety Board’s 
opinion a thorough risk analysis is essential. This is especially true given that the risks were known 
during the admission process (in particular as the result of the trip to Switzerland). The simulation 
results, which were not based on the actual state of the track at that time and which were not 
scientifically sound, did not provide a solid enough basis for concluding that the deployment of this 
long pushed train was safe.  
The Dutch Safety Board is of the opinion that NSR, given its ultimate responsibility for the safety of 
passengers it (NSR), should have been more critical about its use of simulations. One aspect of this 
is that NS Reizigers should have tackled ProRail about the accuracy of the information that the 
latter had provided for the admission testing in its capacity as infrastructure administrator. 
ProRail is responsible for the safety of the infrastructure. In the Dutch Safety Board’s view, this 
certainly includes providing accurate information on the actual state of the infrastructure. ProRail 
failed to do this.  
With regard to the role of the TWMI, the Dutch Safety Board is of the opinion that the Inspectorate 
would have done better to restrict itself to the admission of the steering carriage and should not 
have issued a statement about the train as a whole.

## MEASURES TAKEN AFTER THE DERAILMENT  
5.5 
After the derailment, the TWMI issued a new VGB for the pull/push train. After consulting with NS 
Reizigers and ProRail, a proviso was added to the VGB this time that reduced the special speed 
limit for this train from 30 to 25 kilometres per hour in bends tighter than 190 metres and in 1:8 
and 1:9 sets of points. The Inspectorate also stipulated that no large pushing and pulling forces 
were permitted to occur in points lines.  
This new admission raises a number of issues. After all, at the time it was issued, there were still 
many unanswered questions about the cause of the derailment. This means that there were also 
still some doubts about the part the train’s speed had played in its derailment. Hence, reducing the 
maximum speed offered no guarantees that new derailments could be prevented. While it is true in 
a general sense that lower speeds reduce the forces between wheel and rail, this is no guarantee 
that the risk of derailment will be reduced to an acceptable level. Another point is that the new 
speed limit of 25 kilometres per hour is just as unlikely to be complied with as was the maximum 
speed of 30 kilometres per hour. The ATB system does not work on speeds under 40 kilometres per 
hour, and NS Reizigers did not monitor whether the 30 kilometres per hour speed limit was being 
complied with. 
It was established afterwards that the pushing force had been an important factor. Note that in its 
instructions to engine drivers after the derailment (and after the above-mentioned letter from the 
TWMI), NS Reizigers did say that the drivers must make sure that they do not brake too hard or 
apply the maximum pushing force in points lines. However, there are no technical resources that 
NS Reizigers can use to force compliance with this measure. ProRail cannot exclude the possibility 
either that a combination of tight bends and short interim straight sections can occur on a route. 
This is why it is impossible to rule out that the same combination of factors could occur again, 
leading to a high risk of derailment. In this connection, in November 2006 the Dutch Safety Board 
sent a letter to NS Reizigers and ProRail containing a recommendation that they take measures to 
prevent this combination of factors from re-occurring.  
Following on from the derailment and the investigation into it, NS Reizigers checked out all the 
buffers (incl. their mountings) used on this type of train; it says that it uncovered no irregularities. 
When maintaining this type of train, NS Reizigers also spends extra time on checking the buffer 
lubrication and the coupling method used. 
ProRail states that it has taken the relevant set of points (57A) out of service. After making an 
inventory, another set of points was taken out of service too. As a result, the route taken by the 
derailed train could no longer be used. In addition, after receiving the Dutch Safety Board’s interim 
report in November 2006, ProRail carried out a national inventarisation of locations of S-bends that 
have a radius of less than 200 metres and an enclosed straight section of 12 metres that are used 
by pushed trains. This exercise uncovered about 60 potentially risky situations.  
Three of the locations will be investigated with high priority to see whether any operating measures 
were needed. ProRail and NS Reizigers have now stated in a joint letter that: 
 
this type of equipment would not be permitted to be used at the above-mentioned four 
situations (a measure now implemented) 
 
the relevant engine drivers have been instructed about the maximum speed limit (25 
km/h) and about the pulling and pushing force to be applied (i.e. no more than half the 
maximum force) by the locomotive should re-routing mean that the train does after all 
have to use the above-mentioned locations. 
Operating rules will be agreed, implemented and monitored with the transport provider for the 
remaining 57 locations. ProRail has also let it be known that it will talk to the Transport and Water 
Management Inspectorate to come up with joint measures to improve the procedure for equipment 
admission and the role that the infrastructure administrator plays in this process.

## CONCLUSIONS 
## IMMEDIATE CAUSE 
1. 
Marks at the accident site quickly led us to conclude that the rearmost bogie of the carriage 
in front of the pushing locomotive was the first to derail, doing so to the inner side of the 
bend. The subsequent events were a consequence of this derailment. 
2. 
It has not been possible to establish precisely why the carriage derailed there and in that 
way. After a great deal of investigation using simulations, the Dutch Safety Board has 
established that there was no single cause that can by itself explain the crash. Instead, it was 
a combination of the following factors: tight layout of the infrastructure, large pushing force, 
deployment of a long train, train speed, and possibly one or more buffer defects. It has 
proved impossible to precisely establish and reconstruct the factors that played a decisive 
role in the derailment. However, it is now clear to the Dutch Safety Board that the 
combination of this train (long train being pushed with full force) with this infrastructure 
(tight S-bend, almost no straight section) is risky in the sense that little is needed for the 
train to derail there. 
3. 
The Dutch Safety Board has carried out further investigation into possible defects in the 
train’s buffers, but the available facts and simulations performed have led the Dutch Safety 
Board to conclude that no direct link can be made between the possible failure of a buffer 
and the derailment. The breaking off of one of the buffers was most likely the result of the 
derailment and not the sole or partial cause.  
4. 
In the simulations commissioned by the Dutch Safety Board, the train does not derail. There 
are two possible (non-exclusive) explanations for this. 1. There is an unknown uncertainty in 
the simulation results. This can cause the train to avoid derailment in the simulation even 
though it experienced it in real life. 2. Not all factors have been recognised and/or accurately 
taken into account in the simulation. For instance, potential undiscovered deviations in the 
infrastructure could have played a decisive role in the derailment. Note that these must have 
been minor deviations, as no major deviations (such as broken or rising rails) were found. 
## PRORAIL 
5. 
The infrastructure did not comply with the standards set at the time the track was laid. The 
OVS draft regulations lay down 1:9 set of points as standard, whereas there was a single 1:8 
set of points, which means the train has to turn through a tighter bend. Another point is that 
the interim straight section between this set of points and the following reserve curve was 
not 12 metres long as required by the regulations but maxium 12 metres. Officially, ProRail is 
allowed to deviate from the OVS, provided that the deviation in question has been approved 
in writing by the TEB Manager (see above). ProRail was unable to provide evidence of such 
written approval.  
6. 
These infrastructural dimensions that deviate from the OVS have a negative impact on the 
likelihood of derailment. Simulations indicate that in the situation in question, the forces 
between wheel and rail can fluctuate more greatly (in particular causing greater wheel load 
throw-off) than in an S-bend that does comply with the draft regulations.  
7. 
The precise length of the straight section and the precise radius of the reserve curve were 
not known. ProRail did not have data on the dimensions prior to the derailment, nor was the 
radius measured in the measurements taken after the derailment by ProRail. The Dutch 
Safety Board concludes that ProRail failed to obtain adequate knowledge about the precise 
location of the track. In addition, ProRail has no overview of the sites where the track layout 
deviates from the draft regulations. 
8. 
Little is known about the effect of having a shorter straight section between S-bends. Railway 
experts disagree about the impact that straight sections have on the safety of track sections. 
ProRail lacks knowledge about S-bends in general and about the length of the interim 
straight section in particular, preventing it from accurately assessing the safety of deviating 
track scenarios (such as the one in question).

## NS REIZIGERS 
9.  
The pull-push train with 12 carriages may be defined as a new concept in trains. This train 
configuration is about 50% longer than the previously introduced Benelux train, which has 8 
carriages. NS Reizigers had no experience of the effects of using the longer train. This is why 
NSR commissioned an investigation. This investigation provided some important warnings. 
Swiss Rail’s experiences with long pushed trains revealed that modifications sometimes had 
to be made to such trains and that extensive testing (including practical tests and 
measurements) was needed. 
10.  The follow-up testing for the admission consisted of a practical test and simulations. The 
initial simulation results also led to the recommendation that this train could not be put into 
service ‘just like that’. These results were later shown to have been based on an unrealistic 
assumption about the straight section between two sets of points, namely that this section 
was just 2.3 metres long. A further simulation was then performed using the length of 12 
metres for the straight section quoted by the OVS draft regulations. However, the real-life 
situation in Amsterdam - where there was a straight section no longer than 4 metres 
between the set of points and the reserve curve - has shown that this initial assumption was 
less unrealistic than previously thought. 
11.  The admission testing revealed that a pushed train generates greater forces between wheel 
and rail than does the same train when it is being pulled. The simulations commissioned by 
the Dutch Safety Board confirm this. The greatest forces are generated in the carriage 
immediately in front of the pushing locomotive (which was the first carriage to derail), and 
furthermore at the spot where the carriage actually derailed in practice (i.e. at the start of 
the reserve curve). 
12. 
Simulations are a simplified representation of reality. The precise location of the 
infrastructure was not known in all respects, something that lessens the reliability of the 
results. Another point is that the model has not been validated by actual derailment criteria 
in particular such that we would be able to precisely say how accurately the simulation forces 
correspond to the forces generated in reality. The derailment risk values produced by the 
simulation are close to the maximum values. Given the above-mentioned uncertainty about 
the simulation results, it is realistic to assume that the maximum values could indeed be 
exceeded in practice and/or that little would be needed to make the train derail. 
## THE ADMISSION PROCESS 
13.   The Dutch Safety Board is of the opinion that the admission process relied too heavily on 
simulation results. In situations where investigation makes it clear that there is absolutely no 
danger of safety margins being eroded, it may be enough to work with nothing more than 
simulation results. However, in a situation such as the one under investigation, where the 
risks have already been flagged and where simulation results indicate that safety limits come 
closer to being breached than is the case for other train configurations, then simulation 
results on their own do not offer enough to base an admission process on. Accordingly, the 
conclusion that it was safe to deploy the train should have (also) been based on scientifically 
sound practical tests that would have had to demonstrate that the most important standards 
were not being exceeded. This means there also has to be a discussion about the size of the 
safety margin needed to compensate for any deviations from the norm such as exceeding the 
speed limit or buffer defects.  
14. 
ProRail, which advises on admissions, must guarantee in future that the test will be based on 
the actual state of the track. This was not the case in the admission process looked at. 
Thanks to ProRail’s intercession, the simulations were based on 1:9 sets of points and on a 
straight section that complied with the draft regulations, even though more risky locations 
existed in practice. 
15. 
In this case, the Transport and Water Management Inspectorate (TWMI) played a limited 
role, as only part of the train still needed to pass through the admission process. All the 
same, the TWMI handled this admission testing and issued a temporary admission certificate. 
The Dutch Safety Board believes that it would have been better if the Inspectorate had not 
done this, in order to ensure that the relevant responsibilities remained clearly defined. The

explicitly look at the question of whether the testing provided sufficient justification for 
saying that the safe deployment of this type of train could now be guaranteed, especially in

statement that a train can be safely deployed. 
## FINAL CONCLUSION  
16.  The cause of the derailment on 15 August at Amsterdam Central station proved to be 
complex. It is now clear how and where the train derailed but it is not clear why it derailed 
there and in that way. There is no single factor on its own that can explain the derailment. 
However, what is clear to the Dutch Safety Board is that this type of train, when being 
pushed with full force along the section of track in question, generates large forces. In the 
Board’s opinion, these forces are so great that this combination of train and track must be 
considered undesirable.  
17.  Both in the simulations performed during the admission testing and in the simulations 
performed by the Dutch Safety Board itself, the risk of derailment just complies with the 
internationally agreed standards. The Board feels that this raises the issue of the extent to 
which these simulation models are able to generate results accurate enough to base an 
admission on, when subject to exceptional conditions. In the Dutch Safety Board’s opinion, 
greater caution should have been used, especially given the warnings made about the risks 
of using long pushed trains. In such circumstances, scientifically sound practical tests must 
be performed, in order to determine whether the risk of derailment complies with 
international standards in practice.  
18.  All stakeholders have their own responsibilities in respect of the admission process. NS 
Reizigers is responsible for the safe transportation of passengers and thus for the deployment 
of safe equipment. When deploying a new or modified train configuration, this means that 
the risks must be extensively analysed and any risks must be properly managed. ProRail is 
responsible for maintaining the track in good condition and thus for recognising the actual 
state of the track. This knowledge must then be used when advising on the admission of new 
equipment. The TWMI is responsible for monitoring the safety of the railway system. Based 
on the above conclusions, the Dutch Safety Board is of the opinion that all parties could 
improve the way they discharge their responsibilities in practice. Recommendations in this 
regard are set out in the next section.

The TWMI argues that the facts established mean that greater weight should be given to the

The Dutch Safety Board has carried out simulations that simulated the lack of, and the breaking off 
of, a buffer. Both the draft and final versions of the report state that this did not lead to a 
derailment in the simulation, even if the observed track layout and maximum pushing force are 
taken into account. Despite the stated uncertainty about simulation results, this can be seen as an 
important indication that even if there were defects in the buffer, these did not play a major role in 
the derailment. Another point is that the visual inspection of the bolt found revealed that with a 
probability bordering on certainty its breaking off was consequential damage. No traces of fatigue 
cracks were found in the bolt. Despite the fact that traces of inadequate pre-stress were found in 
one of the connections between buffer and carriage, the Dutch Safety Board has concluded that the 
buffer very probably failed as a result of the derailment, and that even if the buffer did fail prior to 
the derailment then it was a less important factor than was the combination of pushed locomotion, 
tight track layout and maximum driving forces. 
NS Reizigers and the TWMI argue that the admission testing was in fact based on the worst case 
scenario for the track, as simulations were based on maximum errors in altitude and warp and 
because (also in comparison with the simulated errors) the difference between 1:8 sets of points 
and 1:9 sets of points is marginal. 
The Dutch Safety Board believes that not only the 1:8 set of points but also the shorter straight 
section should have been simulated, as both occur in practice. The effects of this should have been 
looked at in conjunction with the above maximum errors. Even if the difference between a 1:8 set 
of points and a 1:9 set of points is not to be measured in dozens of percent, in combination with 
the other factors a few percent can have a significant effect on the risk of derailment. 
NS Reizigers and the Ministry of Transport, Public Works and Water Management both state that 
the fact that the simulation results remained within the derailment thresholds means that we 
should not conclude that the situation is unsafe. 
In the first place, it is the case that the derailment proved that the situation in question was in fact 
unsafe. Subsequently, the situation in question was mapped out as accurately as possible and then 
entered by the Dutch Safety Board in a simulation model. The simulation results indicate that the 
derailment thresholds came close to being achieved (y/q of 0.72 against 0.8 and wheel load throw-
off of 92% against 100%), and furthermore that these peaks were achieved at the exact spot 
where the train actually derailed. If the above-mentioned values had been the result of 
scientifically sound practical tests then it could have been concluded that the situation is safe. 
However, these values have been taken from simulations whose reliability is not precisely known, 

## Página 41

which means such a conclusion is premature. The possibility cannot be excluded that in practice 
the values are higher (and the derailment thresholds exceeded) than shown by the simulation. As 
long as there are such doubts about the safety of the location - and given that a train actually 
derailed there in real life - the Dutch Safety Board will continue to speak of an unsafe situation. 
NS Reizigers indicates that there are indeed a number of factors that generate greater lateral 
forces but that this does not affect the risk of derailment, as the vertical forces increase too, so 
that the y/q value (the most important derailment criterion) stays at about the same level and 
remains below the maximum threshold value. 
It may be true on the whole that lateral forces increase simultaneously with a rise in vertical 
forces, but these two forces are not directly related to each other. It is certainly true when a train 
changes direction quickly (for instance in a tight S-bend) and/or is in pushing mode that both 
forces can fluctuate greatly and can lead to peaks in the y/q value (as shown by the simulations). 
The fact that the train derailed towards the inside of a bend is another clear sign that this was not 
a ‘predictable’ phenomenon where a train’s wheel ascends towards the outside of a bend and 
causes it to derail. The forces fluctuated much more than is the case in a ‘standard curve’. The fact 
that the values in the simulation remain below the threshold value is no guarantee of safety (see 
previous paragraph).  
The results of the simulations performed by the Dutch Safety Board correspond reasonably closely 
to the results of the simulations that NS Reizigers commissioned. NS Reizigers claims that this 
means that we can be confident that the calculations are accurate.  
The Dutch Safety Board is of the opinion that it is evident that both simulation models used 
produce similarly (in)accurate results. Both are based on the latest simulation techniques. 
However, the Dutch Safety Board has no precise data about either the model’s accuracy or its 
reliability. Both models have been validated in general terms for this scenario, but not specifically 
for it, which means that the results must be viewed with the necessary caution, something 
especially true of the derailment risk. The Board is of the opinion that the results do indicate 
whether a situation is riskier or less risky, but individually do not provide any evidence of whether 
a particular situation is safe. More measurement data would be needed for this.

## APPENDIX 3: ASSESSMENT FRAMEWORK USED BY THE DUTCH SAFETY BOARD 
In principle, there are several possible ways of testing and assessing the way in which an 
organisation discharges its own responsibility for safety in practice. This also means that there is 
no universal guide that can be used in all circumstances. This is despite the fact that since the 
1990s there has been an increasing focus on own responsibility for safety. Accordingly, the Dutch 
Safety Board has itself selected five safety action items that give some idea of the factors that 
could be important and their relative importance. The Dutch Safety Board believes that this 
selection is justified, because these safety-related action items have been incorporated into 
numerous pieces of national and international legislation and regulations, as well as into a large 
number of broadly accepted and broadly implemented standards. The Dutch Safety Board’s list of 
action items is as follows:  
1. Safety policy based on understanding risks  
Starting points for the attainment of the required level of safety are:  
 
an investigation of the system, followed by  
 
an inventarisation of the associated risks.  
This step is used to determine which hazards need to be managed and which preventive and 
repressive measures are needed in this context.  
2. Demonstrable and realistic safety policy  
In order to prevent and manage undesirable events, a realistic safety policy implementable in 
practice, including the associated starting points, needs to be laid down. This safety policy must be 
established at and guided from managerial level. This safety policy is based on:  
 
relevant current legislation and regulations 
 
available standards, guidelines and ‘best practices’ from the industry, on own insights and 
experiences of the organisation in question, and on the specific safety goals set for the 
organisation.  
3. Implementing and enforcing safety policy  
The implementation and enforcement of the safety policy and the management of the identified 
risks is achieved by:  
 
drawing up a description of the way in which the safety policy will be implemented, 
including specific objectives and plans and the resulting preventive and repressive 
measures.  
 
drawing up transparent, unambiguous and ‘accessible to all’ allocation of responsibilities on 
the workfloor for the implementation and enforcement of safety plans and measures.  
 
clear definition of the personnel deployment and expertise required for the various tasks.  
 
a clear and active centralised coordination of safety activities.  
4. Tightening up the safety policy  
The safety policy must be continually tightened up, based on:  
 
the periodical - including for each amendment of the starting points - performance of (risk 
and other) analyses, observations, inspections and audits (proactive approach).  
 
a system of monitoring and investigation of incidents, near-accidents and accidents, along 
with an expert analysis of them (reactive approach).  
On this basis, evaluations will be performed and the safety policy may be modified by the 
management team, if necessary. Possible improvements will be revealed too and can be actively 
implemented.  
5. Management guidance, involvement and communication 
The management team of the stakeholders/organisation must:  
 
be responsible internally for setting clear and realistic expectations regarding the safety 
objectives, and for ensuring that there is a climate of continuous improvement in safety on 
the workfloor, including by always setting a good example, and last but not least by 
providing sufficient people and resources.  
 
communicate clearly externally regarding the general procedure, test methods, deviation 
procedures etc., based on clear arrangements made with the external environment that are 
laid down in writing.

Radius and reserve curve 
There are still some unanswered questions about the radius of the reserve curve. The design 
drawings indicate that the radius is 200 metres, but it is not known whether a bend with a radius of 
200 metres was actually laid there. There is no ‘as built’ drawing of the yard nor does ProRail have 
any measurements pertaining to this bend. After the derailment, ProRail carried out lots of 
measurements of the infrastructure but unfortunately did not measure the radius of this reserve 
curve. ProRail has stated in discussions that it should have done this but failed to do so. ProRail did 
have an aerial photo of the yard taken before the accident, which permits a rough estimate to be 
made of the radius. ProRail itself concludes that the radius was very probably at least 190 metres. 
The Dutch Safety Board has used this value in its investigation.  
The track section was restored after the derailment. Before it was put back into service, 
measurements were taken, including of the reserve curve in question. It turned out that this was a 
‘clothoid-like bend’ whose radius varied from about 300 to 150 metres. Before being put back into 
service, this bend was modified and given an approximately constant radius of 200 metres.  
There are also unanswered questions about the length of the interim straight section between the 
set of points and the reserve curve. Here too, this section was not measured after it was laid or 
after the derailment. Accordingly, for this value too the Dutch Safety Board had to use the above 
aerial photo. The straight section between the set of points and the reserve curve was estimated at 
between 0 and 4 metres in length (excluding the approximately 4 metres of length built into the 
set of points as standard).  
Legislation on bends and sets of points 
At the time of the accident, the new Railways Act was already in force. Article 8 of the Regulation 
on the main railway infrastructure is relevant here. This Article states that for a speed of 40 
kilometres per hour the bend radius must be at least 190 metres. The radius in which the first 
signs of derailment were found probably did comply with this requirement, if ProRail’s estimate is 
accurate. However, as stated above, we can no longer be absolutely certain about this.  
Sets of points with a crossing angle ratio of 1:8 do not actually comply with the new Railways Act, 
as these have a radius of 184 metres. Accordingly, this was also true of the set of points 57A that 
the train went through. 
However, the explanatory notes to the Act reveal that the regulations in question only apply to new 
structures and not with retroactive effect to the existing track. At the time that the track in 
question was laid, the OVS draft regulations on the railway infrastructure were in force, which 
require minimum radii of 160 metres.  
Legislation on straight sections 
The new Railways Act contains a provision regarding the minimum length of straight section 
between two bends. Article 8 of the Regulation on the main railway infrastructure states that after 
a change of direction the available stabilisation time must be at least 2 seconds (which at 40 
km/hour translates into 24 metres of track). The track section in question, at about 3.5 metres in 
length, was too short to comply with this. However, as stated, the relevant regulation only applies 
to new infrastructure.  
With regard to the ‘old’ regulations, in this connection the ‘ontwerpvoorschrift wissels en 
kruisingen’ (‘draft regulation on sets of points and line crossings’ (OVS00056-6.1) applies. This 
draft regulation dates from 1 July 2003 and includes a requirement of 1 second (which at 40 

## Página 48

km/hour translates into 12 metres of track). The track section in question failed to comply with this 
requirement either. Officially, ProRail is allowed to deviate from the OVS, provided that this has 
been agreed in writing by the Manager TEB (Technisch-Economisch-Beheer/technical and economic 
administration). ProRail was unable to provide a copy of this written approval. 
Discussion on the straight sections’ effect on safety 
The Dutch Safety Board has carried out further investigation into the importance of the length of 
straight sections between bends. In this work, it was advised by Professor Esveld of the Delft 
Technical University. The information obtained was as follows: the length of the straight section 
between two bends affects passenger comfort and safety. With regard to passenger comfort, the 
shorter the straight section, the more rapidly that passengers are subjected to opposing transverse 
forces, making them feel less comfortable. With regard to safety, the effect of a shorter straight 
section is twofold: the relative buffer displacements increase and the transverse forces increase 
too. The first increases the likelihood of buffer bypass and the second increases the risk of 
derailment.  
Both the level of comfort and the risk of derailment depend on many factors other than simply the 
length of the straight section between two bends. For example, the risk of buffer bypass and 
derailment also depends on the size of the longitudinal forces and on the direction that they work 
in. For trains being pushed with maximum pushing force, the above factors reinforce each other. It 
is also true that the negative effect of a shorter straight section increases as the train speed 
increases.  
In terms of both comfort and safety, then, the precise negative effect of short straight sections is 
not clear-cut (and accordingly nor is the minimum length to be used). According to the OVS, the 
safety aspect is the most critical one and thus is the decisive factor for determining the minimum 
prescribed length. However, according to Professor Esveld, in this context it is the comfort factor 
that is the more critical and thus the one that determines the standards set in relation to the 
straight section between bends. However, this does not alter the fact that a (too) short straight 
section also has a negative impact on safety. As stated, the degree to which this is the case 
depends on other factors.  
The following comments on the length of the straight section between bends should also be noted: 
 
the information provided by Professor Esveld and others reveals that evidently there are no 
clear-cut safety requirements for straight sections 
 
the simulations have shown that reducing the length of the straight section has a 
significant effect on the relative buffer displacements. In the complete absence of a straight 
section, these can be as much as 55 cm or so. This is very significant, given that the 
buffers in question are 60 cm wide. The simulations also indicate that the wheel load 
throw-off is greater than in the longer (12-metre) straight section required by the OVS 
draft regulations.

General 
The simulations for this investigation were performed using the simulation programme ´Simpack´. 
This is a programme used in many countries to help develop and test railway equipment, which is 
also used for applications in other sectors.  
The validity of the model used for this Intercity train was tested by comparing simulation results 
with measurements from practical tests performed for this type of Intercity train. This comparison 
reveals that in essence the measurements and simulation results are in line with each other. There 
are differences in the finer details, part of which can be explained by track irregularities that 
affected the practical test measurements. The validation is insufficient to quantify the reliability and 
accuracy of the model. 
Simulations were used to investigate the effect of a number of factors. A reference point used was 
a simulation that was based on values derived from information on the locomotive and steering 
carriage that was taken from the ARR automatic journey recording system. This data reveals that 
the train’s speed at the moment of derailment was about 39 kilometres per hour and that the 
locomotive was supplying a large pushing force at the moment of derailment (with the longitudinal 
forces resulting from the pushing being estimated at 200 kN). For the rest, the simulation was 
based as far as possible on the derailment site: 1:8 set of points leading to the right, 3.5 metres 
straight section and reserve curve with radius of 190 metres. As stated above, it proved impossible 
to reconstruct the precise layout of the track.  
Pushing versus pulling 
The first step was to compare this simulation with a simulation in which the same train travelling at 
the same speed was not pushed but pulled. The results confirm the (logical) expectation that 
pushing generates certain greater forces than does pulling. For instance, considerably greater 
friction forces occur between the buffers (36% increase). In addition, the maximum Y/Q ratio for 
each axle is greater (0.7 instead of 0.6), but note that the most commonly used threshold value of 
0.85 is not exceeded. The maximum buffer displacements increase too (by 22%, from 410 mm to 
500 mm). Finally, the wheel load throw-off when pushing is 68% higher than when pulling.  
The pushing forces  
Investigation of the ARR reveals that at the moment of derailment, the locomotive may have been 
pushing with maximum pushing force. This may mean that the starting tractive power of the 
derailed train reached a peak load of about 250 kN. The ´base simulation´ assumed a conservative 
200 kN. In order to look at the effect of greater forces, a simulation was also performed based on 
300 kN of starting tractive power. In this case, the various forces become much greater. The forces 
acting on and between the buffers increase and persist for longer. The transverse forces become 
more critical. The most critical in particular is the rearmost axle of the rearmost bogie (which was 
the first bogie to derail).  
In the interim bend, where the derailment occurred, there was almost full wheel load throw-off 
(92%) of the left wheel of the fourth axle. However, it is true that at that moment the set of 
wheels were more on the right side of the track, so that there was no risk of the left wheel 
‘climbing up’ the rail. The right-hand wheel of the fourth axle briefly has a high Y/Q value (0.88). 
This value lies in the danger zone - it is just that the peak would have to persist longer for a 
derailment to result. It must also be remembered that the derailment did not occur to the right.  
Buffer friction  
The simulation results outlined above had already revealed that pushing causes the buffer forces 
and buffer displacements to increase sharply. This is why a simulation was performed that 
assumed a higher friction coefficient. The friction could be greater for instance if the buffer plate 
was damaged or if the buffers were inadequately lubricated. The simulation revealed that an 
increased friction coefficient (0.2 instead of 0.1) also increases the friction forces. It is worth noting 
that this makes the set of wheels on the left-hand side of the track continue to run for a longer 
period, even though the moment when this set starts running on the left and the time of the

above-mentioned wheel load throw-off of the left wheel still do not coincide. However, the 
increased buffer friction does make the situation more critical. 
Track design  
The exact radius of the reserve curve in which the train derailed is not known (see section 3.3.1). 
Accordingly, a simulation was performed using a bend radius of 180 metres instead of the 
estimated 190 metres. This revealed that a smaller radius has little effect on the forces between 
wheel and rail, although the buffer displacements did increase (to 520 mm instead of 500 mm). A 
simulation that assumed no interim straight section at all (0 instead of 3.5 metres) revealed that 
the buffer displacements increased to a maximum 550 mm, leading to a real risk of buffer bypass. 
This sharper bend had little effect on wheel contact. 
The effect of track irregularities such as those measured by ProRail after the derailment - including 
a minor banking of max. 2 mm and a railway gauge of 1442 mm - were simulated too. These 
irregularities hardly affect the forces at all. 
In order to gain a better understanding of the effect of a short - or complete absence of a - straight 
section in combination with tight bends, a simulation was performed where the track layout 
complied with the design requirements set out in 2003, namely a 1:9 set of points and a 
cushioning zone of 12 metres. This affected the buffer displacements in particular, which reduced 
by half (250 mm instead of 500 mm). There was also considerably less wheel load throw-off in the 
set of points, but not in the reserve curve.  
Bolster leeway threshold 
Intercity carriages are equipped with curve-dependent bolster leeway thresholds. These are rigid 
bumpers that limit the movements made by a carriage body vis-à-vis a bogie. The bumpers are 
rigid (steel on steel), which can mean that a jolt may be accompanied by a counter-jolt. The 
investigation of marks left after the derailment also observed significant damage to the bumpers. 
The simulation revealed that in the situation looked at, the bolster leeway thresholds hit each 
other. This happens in all sets of points, but the jolt is especially hard at the boundary of the 
rearmost bogie of the derailed carriage when driving through the joining curve (where the 
derailment occurred). The jolting creates additional wheel load throw-off with the left-hand wheels, 
which makes the situation more critical. When the train is pushed, the bumpers come into play 
more readily than is the case when the train is pulled.  
Finally, the effects of tautly connected carriages (which were not present in this case), was looked 
at, but this scarcely affected the interplay of forces between wheel and rail.  
Further investigation into the buffers 
The simulation results clearly show that fairly significant buffer displacements occur in the reserve 
curve where the train derailed. This is why both NS Reizigers and the Dutch Safety Board re-
examined the derailed train’s buffers. When the photographic material was re-examined, it was 
noted again that a buffer on the penultimate carriage had part of its mountings broken off. It had 
been initially assumed (partly because it was not a buffer on the derailed carriage) that this was 
consequential damage, but in theory the breaking off of the buffer could have contributed to the 
derailment.  
It is not impossible that the buffer broke off close to the derailment or shortly before it, as at that 
moment the buffer displacement was at its maximum and thus the eccentric load on the left-hand 
side of the buffer was at its greatest.  
The parties also considered whether the buffer could have broken off as the result of buffer bypass 
(i.e. when two buffers catch behind each other). This is a possibility, but only as the result of buffer 
bypass that was itself due to the derailment. Buffer bypass as the cause of the derailment is very 
unlikely. The traces of buffer bypass found on the buffers indicate a buffer bypass on the right-
hand side of the broken-off buffer (which could well be due to consequential damage), whereas 
buffer bypass near the derailment site would be expected on the left-hand side of this buffer.  
Broken-off buffer 
In the simulation model, the breaking off of the buffer was simulated as accurately as possible. 
The effect of this was simulated in two ways. First, we looked at the forces that occur when a 
single buffer is missing or no longer delivers any counterforce. In that case, the forces are passed 
on well by the buffers on the other side. This makes little difference to the forces between wheel 
and rail.  

## Página 51

Secondly, an attempt was made to simulate the situation where the buffer suddenly breaks off just 
before the moment where the train derails in practice. In that case too, the buffer pair on the other 
side appears to adequately assume the ´task´ performed by the missing buffer. The buffers in the 
right-hand pair are not touching each other due to the tight radius, which does create some 
dynamic forces (a one-off ‘jolt’ when they ‘bang’ on each other), but these dynamic forces do not 
translate into larger forces in the wheel-rail contact. What does happen, however, is that the 
rearmost wheels of the final carriage stay longer on the left-hand side of the track. This does make 
the situation more critical, as there is almost complete wheel load throw-off on the left-hand side. 
This can cause the wheel to lift up and go over the rail. By the way, this does not happen in the 
simulation. 
Buffer crossing  
The large buffer displacements that occur lead to a risk of so-called ‘buffer crossing’. In the event 
of a large buffer displacement (in the simulations between 500 and 550 mm) the buffers are 
subjected to heavy eccentric loads, which could cause them to get stuck and not spring in any 
more (known as ‘buffer crossing’). This theoretical possibility was investigated too via a simulation. 
This revealed that this effect actually occurred in the simulation, at about the spot where the train 
derailed and for the buffer that broke off. This crossing affects the forces. The development of the 
vertical forces becomes more dynamic, but does not appear to have any decisive effect on the risk 
of derailment.

Admission testing  
Between 2000 and 2003, NS Reizigers mandated Nedtrain Consulting29 (NTC) to carry out research 
into the possible deployment of a pull-push train consisting of a locomotive (type 1700), a steering 
carriage at the other end and eleven carriages in between. The locomotive and the carriages were 
already being used and thus had already been admitted for use on the railway. However, the 
deployment of the train would change when it was pushed, which is why a TWMI admission 
certificate was still required. However, this was not the first time that pushed trains had been 
used: for example, the Benelux train has already been deployed as a pull-push train since the 
1970s, with the same Intercity carriages, although note that the Benelux train is much shorter 
(205 metres compared to 340 metres), lighter (370 tonnes compared to 570 tonnes) and uses a 
less powerful locomotive.  
NTC visited the Swiss railways to obtain more information about the latter’s experiences with 
pushed trains. These discussions revealed that using long pushed trains is not without risk and that 
such trains should not be introduced until extensive practical testing had been carried out. In 
Switzerland, many modifications had been made to the trains to permit safe pushing with long 
trains, such as reinforcing the buffers, having the buffers attached more solidly to the vehicle 
housing, and restricting the locomotive’s pushing power. 
A practical test was then carried out in the yard in Zwolle (NL), in which a pull/push train was 
driven at various speeds – both in braking and pushing mode – through two 1:8 sets of points 
(with an approximately 180 metres radius). These tests measured such factors as the relative 
buffer displacements, the forces between the sets of wheels and bogie frame, and various lateral 
accelerations. These measurements established that the lateral rail forces (the so-called ‘H forces’) 
had exceeded the norm for these forces by 8%.   
NTC then simulated the performance of this pull/push train. First, the simulation model was 
compared with the results from the Zwolle practical tests. NTC concluded from these that the 
model was sufficiently valid to be able to generate reliable simulation results. NTC then made the 
virtual train run through a 1:9 set of points that had a 200 metre radius. NTC also varied and 
tested various conditions (such as friction between buffers and train length). At ProRail’s request, 
the simulation used 1:9 sets of points and not the tighter 1:8 sets of points because - according to 
ProRail - 1:8 sets of points occurred rarely in practice and because over time they would all be 
replaced by 1:9 sets of points or by even more spacious points.  
There was some more discussion during the investigation into the layout of a 1:9. Initially, NTC 
used data on a 1:9 set of points from the OVS draft regulations in the simulation, without including 
the accompanying straight section in the model. Based on these simulations, NTC concluded in 
March 2002 that the introduction of a pushed train with 12 carriages would not be safe ‘just like 
that’. A number of safety norms were exceeded, both in the practical tests and in the simulations. 
NTC recommended that either the number of carriages be limited to 8 or that the pushing force of 
the locomotive be limited to 60% when driving through points lines. As an alternative, NTC also

Subsequently, ProRail submitted information on the straight section that is built into a 1:9 set of

At the time of the admission of the train, the RAILNED ‘M001 standard’ requirements applied. 
These are the same requirements that new equipment applying for admission must satisfy. 
However, this standard does not contain any criteria relating to the safe driving of carriages in 
push mode. This is why NTC decided to bring the test into line with the international standards UIC 
518 (trackload and dynamic risk of derailment) and the ERRI standards for static risk of





Conclusions from admission testing 
NTC came to the conclusion that the criterion for H-forces would indeed be exceeded, whereas the 
standard for the Y/Q ratio would not. 
In the final simulation performed by NTC, the H-forces (i.e. the lateral forces) are 40 kN for an 
empty train (where the standard value is 39 kN) and for a train carrying its maximum load 43.3 kN 
(where the standard value is 44 kN). In an unloaded state, this equals an exceeding of the norm by 
2.5%. This exceeding was observed for the carriage in front of the locomotive at a speed of 44 
kilometres per hour and at maximum pushing force.  
In the earlier defective simulations that used the ‘sets of points without a straight section’, in both 
cases (laden and unladen) there was substantially greater exceeding of the H-forces criterion to the 
tune of 20% and even in some cases to the tune of more than 30% (for a train travelling at 40 
kilometres per hour with maximum pushing force).  
The standard for the Y/Q ratio (0.8) was not exceeded in the final simulations (for a 1:9 set of 
points according to ProRail). The highest Y/Q value measured was 0.71, a safety margin of 10% of 
the norm. In the earlier defective simulations (which used sets of points without a straight section), 
this standard was exceeded, however. These simulations recorded Y/Q values varying from 0.61 to 
1.01. 
NTC concluded that the simulation results show that Intercity rolling stock with a train length of up 
to 12 carriages could safely be deployed in pull/push mode. NTC deemed the exceeding of the 
standard for lateral rail forces by 2.5% to be marginal and permissible, as this was based on the 
worst case scenario (100% traction, including defective track, driving through tight bends with 
radius of 195 metres). The criterion for lateral rail forces is not a derailment criterion either, 
instead being a criterion used to measure comfort and wear and tear. 
Discussion between NSR and ProRail 
Based on the above-mentioned conclusion, NTC proposed that the pull/push combination be 
admitted for deployment. ProRail (which advises the Inspectorate) recommended on the basis of 
the NTC report that the train be admitted but that the speed in sets of points with tight bends 
(radius less than 190 metres and for all 1:8 and 1:7 sets of points) be limited to 30 kilometres per 
hour. There was then some discussion between NSR and ProRail as to the feasibility of enforcing 
this speed limit. This was because NSR wanted this maximum speed to be properly indicated at 
each relevant set of points. However, this did not fit in with ProRail’s existing system of signalling 
and signs. In the end, it was decided to declare that the 30 kilometre per hour speed limit applied 
(but only in pushed mode) in the entire yard at Amsterdam. The tightest sets of points are located 
in this yard, with the tight sets of points located outside Amsterdam not being located on the main 
Maastricht – Haarlem route.
